The rename that erases a category

The CFPB renamed its product categories twice, in April 2017 and August 2023. Both are hard cutovers: the old label stops the day the new one starts.

Chart the label as published and credit reporting appears to collapse to zero in 2024 — the year it tripled. August 2023 carries both labels at once, so a naive total double-counts it.

The credit card family moved in both directions: “Credit card” and “Prepaid card” were merged in 2017, then split apart again in 2023. A split cannot be undone — the 2017–2023 rows never recorded which half they belong to — so all three collapse to the union label. Mapping to the coarser category is the only direction that does not invent information.

Two labels are deliberately left unmapped. “Consumer Loan” retired in 2017 with no clean successor; mapping it to “Vehicle loan or lease” would move 31,559 complaints into a category they may not belong to.

What the data says

  • What you complain about predicts the outcome better than who you complain about. Credit card and checking-account complaints close with money about 16% of the time. Credit reporting complaints do so 0.05% of the time — roughly 1 in 2,000.
  • The headline number is an artifact of mix. Across the file, 1.28% of complaints close with money. That figure is almost entirely a statement about credit reporting, which is 81.75% of every complaint filed. Excluding it, the rate is 6.69%.
  • “The credit bureaus” are not one actor. They take 78% of all complaints between them and effectively never pay money — but TransUnion resolves 59.46% of complaints with some form of relief against Experian’s 12.42%, on comparable volume and the same kinds of complaint.
  • Volume rose twelvefold in five years, from 444,213 in 2020 to 5,442,962 in 2025, and essentially all of the growth is credit reporting.

Two metrics that do not discriminate

Timely response is 99.37% across 17.9 million complaints, and close to 100% at every large company. Response lag has a median and a 90th percentile of 0 days: 93.81% of complaints reach the company the same day.

Neither belongs on a dashboard. A tile that never moves teaches a reader nothing, and building one anyway is how a dashboard fills up with numbers nobody acts on. Reporting that a metric does not discriminate is a finding, not a failure — and it is the finding that decides what the dashboard should show instead.

Proving a check can fail

A check that has never failed is a claim, not a test.

The check this project turns on is “no product series dies mid-file and resurrects.” Run against the published labels it returns seven broken series — Credit card and Prepaid card both die in April 2017 and resurrect in August 2023, exactly the merge-then-split. Run against the mapped labels it returns nothing.

It does not catch everything, and saying otherwise would be worse than the gap. A one-way rename — like credit reporting, where the old label dies and never returns — produces no resurrection to detect. That case is caught by a different check: any label outside the approved vocabulary fails the build. Two checks, two kinds of rename. Two of the seven hits are also false positives from sparsity, where a category has so few complaints that ordinary empty months look like a gap.

What the first build got wrong

The first version declared foreign keys from the fact table to all four dimensions. That costs four index lookups per row, and on 17.9 million rows the insert ran for two and a half hours without finishing. It also mapped the response categories with an UPDATE over every row after loading them, rewriting the whole table to set three columns.

Both are now done differently: no foreign keys during the load — the validation suite checks for orphan keys directly, catching the same defect without paying for it on every row — and the mapping is applied in the insert. The same build now takes 762 seconds.

Honest scope

  • One row per complaint, not per consumer. The same person filing twice is two rows, and the file gives no way to link them.
  • Complaint counts are not normalised. A company with more customers receives more complaints, and this file carries no customer or account counts. Every company comparison here is a rate.
  • Relief is the company’s own characterisation of the outcome; the amount is not published.
  • A complaint is not a finding of fault. The CFPB does not verify the allegations.
  • Company rows require 500+ complaints; below that a single case moves the rate by points.
